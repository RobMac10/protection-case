The 1787 instructions, the Aborigines Act 1905 (WA), and A. O. Neville

Protection, 1787–1937

They called it protection.

St James’s Palace, London
25 April 1787

You are to endeavour by every possible means to open an Intercourse with the Natives and to conciliate their affections, enjoining all Our Subjects to live in amity and kindness with them. And if any of Our Subjects shall wantonly destroy them, or give them any unnecessary Interruption in the exercise of their several occupations. It is our Will and Pleasure that you do cause such offenders to be brought to punishment according to the degree of the Offence.

Western Australia
Reserved 23 December 1905 · in force 20 April 1906

An Act to make provision for the better protection and care of the Aboriginal inhabitants of Western Australia.

The Chief Protector shall be the legal guardian of every aboriginal and half-caste child until such child attains the age of sixteen years.

Canberra
21 to 23 April 1937

The line “They called it protection.” is this file’s frame. It is not a quotation from 1787, or from 1905, or from 1937. The other wording is from those dates.

Case file: Protection / 1787–1937

Date of incident   25 April 1787 · Aborigines Act 1905 (WA), in force 20 April 1906 · deputation 9 March 1928 · Canberra conference 21–23 April 1937

Location   St James’s, London · Western Australia, including Perth and the Moore River Native Settlement · Canberra

Status. The word was “protection.” Whether it ever meant protection is still the question.

What is agreed

  • On 25 April 1787, at St James’s, instructions were issued to Arthur Phillip. They told him to “conciliate their affections,” to enjoin subjects to “live in amity and kindness with them,” and to punish anyone who would “wantonly destroy them.” The same instructions also asked how intercourse “may be turned to the advantage of this country.”
  • The Parliament of Western Australia passed “An Act to make provision for the better protection and care of the Aboriginal inhabitants of Western Australia,” reserved on 23 December 1905, with royal assent proclaimed on 27 April 1906, and proclaimed to commence on 20 April 1906. Section 4 charged a department with “promoting the welfare of the aborigines” and “generally assisting in the preservation and well-being of the aborigines.” Section 8, as enacted in 1905, said: “The Chief Protector shall be the legal guardian of every aboriginal and half-caste child until such child attains the age of sixteen years.” A 1911 amendment added words about the exclusion of the rights of the mother of an illegitimate half-caste child. This file does not print the 1911 words as if they had been in the 1905 Act on the day it was reserved.
  • Auber Octavius Neville was Chief Protector of Aborigines in Western Australia from 1915 to 1936, and Commissioner of Native Affairs from 1936 until he retired in 1940. The Moore River Native Settlement was established in 1918, under the 1905 Act.
  • On 9 March 1928 William Harris, a Noongar man, led an Aboriginal deputation to Premier Philip Collier in Perth. They asked for the 1905 Act to be repealed, for equal rights, and for Moore River to be closed. The Act was not repealed at that meeting.
  • At Canberra, from 21 to 23 April 1937, a conference of Commonwealth and State Aboriginal authorities heard Neville, by then Commissioner of Native Affairs, and recorded both his speech and a resolution on “absorption.” The wording on the typographic page is from that official report.
  • The words “aboriginal” and “half-caste” in the quoted Act and report are the record’s words, not this file’s chosen language for people now.

Three witnesses

The same three questions for each. Phillip and Neville are shown in public-domain portraits from their lifetimes. William Harris is shown as a silhouette, not a likeness. The 1928 deputation photograph is not used here. No likeness has been invented.

Arthur Phillip

Received the 1787 instructions · First Governor of New South Wales, 1788–1792 · Died 1814

The words on the typographic page from 1787 were not his invention. They were the Crown’s instructions to him, given at St James’s on 25 April 1787, before the First Fleet sailed.

What they said. The instructions told him to “conciliate their affections,” to enjoin all subjects “to live in amity and kindness with them,” and to bring to punishment anyone who would “wantonly destroy them, or give them any unnecessary Interruption in the exercise of their several occupations.” In the next sentences the same instructions also asked him to report how “Intercourse with these people may be turned to the advantage of this country.”

Could they really have seen it? He could read the instructions he was given. He could not have seen the Aborigines Act 1905, the office of Chief Protector in Western Australia, or the Moore River Native Settlement. He was dead long before those things existed. This file does not make him a witness to 1905, and it does not claim that he kept the promise in the instructions.

Why might they say it? The words were an official order, and they have been quoted ever since as evidence of a founding promise. Students can ask what “protection” sounded like in 1787, who it was for, and what the same document asked to be got from the people it named.

A. O. Neville

Chief Protector of Aborigines, Western Australia, 1915–1936 · Commissioner of Native Affairs, 1936–1940 · Died 1954

He was the official whose title was “Protector,” and later “Commissioner,” under Western Australian law that had been passed in the name of protection and care.

What they said. At the 1937 conference he asked: “Are we going to have a population of 1,000,000 blacks in the Commonwealth or are we going to merge them into our white community and eventually forget that there ever were any aborigines in Australia?” In the same speech he said: “To achieve this end, however, we must have charge of the children at the age of six years; it is useless to wait until they are twelve or thirteen years of age. In Western Australia we have power under the act to take any child from its mother at any stage of its life, no matter whether the mother be legally married or not.” The report also records the conference resolution that “the destiny of the natives of aboriginal origin, but not of the full blood, lies in their ultimate absorption by the people of the Commonwealth.”

Could they really have seen it? He was Chief Protector, then Commissioner, and he was in the room in Canberra when the official report was made. He could speak about the Act he administered, and about the policy he wanted. He could not speak for the people the Act named, and he could not see, from that conference table, every life lived under it. His language is the language of 1937 official policy. This file keeps it, because it is what he said. It is not this file’s language.

Why might they say it? To argue for a long-range policy of absorption, and to defend the powers already in the Western Australian Act. A man called Protector was describing the taking of children as a means to “merge” and “forget.” Students can ask what the title “Protector” was doing in that sentence.

William Harris

Noongar activist · Led the 1928 Aboriginal deputation to the Premier of Western Australia · 1867–1931

On 9 March 1928 he led the first Western Australian Aboriginal deputation to Premier Philip Collier, to fight for better rights and for the repeal of the Aborigines Act 1905.

What they said. This file does not invent his wording. What can be agreed from the checked record is the deputation itself. On 9 March 1928 Harris led Aboriginal men to the Premier of Western Australia. They asked for the 1905 Act to be repealed, for the same rights as other people, and for the Moore River Native Settlement to be closed, because families were being split and people were held there under police control. For years he had fought that Act. This file leaves his exact spoken sentences to the March 1928 newspapers, which students and teachers can still open.

Could they really have seen it? He was there. He had lived under the Act, and he had organised the deputation. He could speak about what the law and the department were doing to people he knew, including at Moore River. He was not in every district, and one meeting could not change the Act by itself. Premier Collier is later often quoted as speaking of a “great obligation to do justice to the Aboriginal, because [the white man] had deprived him of his country.” That wording should be checked in the original report. Legislative change did not come then.

Why might they say it? To have the Act repealed, and to be heard as men with a case, not as people spoken for by a Protector. Students can ask why a deputation had to argue, in 1928, that “protection” was not protecting them, and what happened after the Premier heard them.

    Super Sleuths

    Go further only if you want to. Teachers should follow AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols wherever this file touches First Nations histories.

    1. “Savages” and “Natives” in the 1787 manuscript

    The typographic page follows the Documenting a Democracy transcription of the 25 April 1787 instructions, and uses the standing word “Natives.” That transcription prints “Savages Natives,” which usually means the handwritten original had a change. Historical Records of Australia prints “natives.” This file does not print “Savages” as if it were the chosen word.

    Question. Why might a clerk strike one word and write another, and what is lost if a later copy hides the change?

    2. The 1911 words that were not in the 1905 Act as reserved

    Section 8, as reserved in 1905, made the Chief Protector the legal guardian of every aboriginal and half-caste child until the age of sixteen. The 1911 amendment (No. 42 of 1911) added “to the exclusion of the rights of the mother of an illegitimate half-caste child.” Some later printings set those words in the same section without making the date of the change clear.

    Question. What changes when a later clause is silently folded into an earlier Act?

    3. William Harris’s own sentences

    This file agrees the deputation happened, who went, and what they asked, from the Australian Dictionary of Biography and the 1928 photograph caption. Contemporary reports in The West Australian and The Daily News of March 1928 should still be read word for word. Later articles quote those papers in reported speech. This file does not turn that reported speech into a first-person invention.

    Question. Why might a later writer change “they claimed” into “I said,” and what is lost when that happens?

    4. Neville’s 1937 words, and later retellings

    The typographic close line, and the sentences about taking children, are from the official 1937 conference report. They are often shortened later. Check the report itself, including the conference resolution on “absorption,” before trusting a later one-line version.

    Question. What does the full page do that a single famous sentence does not?

    5. Moore River, with care

    The Moore River Native Settlement (Mogumber) opened in 1918 under the 1905 Act. It is a place of removal, family separation, and later testimony. This file names it because the 1928 deputation asked for it to be closed. It does not invent a day in the settlement, and it does not print photographs of children. If a class takes this further, it should do so with the relevant Aboriginal communities, and with AIATSIS guidance.

    Question. What can a law and a deputation show, and what can they not show, about a place like Moore River?

    6. Other Protection Acts

    New South Wales had an Aborigines Protection Board from 1883 and an Aborigines Protection Act in 1909. Queensland’s 1897 Act is often treated as a model. This file’s sharp focus is Western Australia, because the 1905 Act, Neville, Moore River, and the 1928 deputation can be held in one frame. The other Acts still matter.

    Question. If “protection” was a word used in more than one colony, what would you need in order to answer the case question for the whole continent?

    7. Premier Collier’s “great obligation”

    Later accounts say Collier recognised “a great obligation to do justice to the Aboriginal, because [the white man] had deprived him of his country.” That line should be checked against the March 1928 newspapers. The Act was not changed at that meeting.

    Question. What is the difference between a recorded promise of consideration, and a change in the law?

    8. Who is missing from the three cards

    The typographic page and the witness cards are official words, and the words of one organised deputation of men. Women and children who lived under the Act are not given invented voices here. A later proposed case, Bringing Them Home, 1997, is the place for the national inquiry. This file leaves a gap on purpose.

    Question. What does a gap in a case file ask you to do, and what does it ask you not to do?

    Teacher Note

    Teachers should follow AIATSIS guidance and local community protocols. Teach this case with the relevant Aboriginal communities — including Noongar communities, and those connected with Moore River / Mogumber — and with AIATSIS guidance. Do not treat a student finding as a substitute for community protocols.

    Curriculum. Australian Curriculum: History v9, Year 10. AC9HH10K09: the causes of First Nations Australians’ campaigns for rights and freedoms before 1965, such as discriminatory legislation and policies, the 1938 Day of Mourning and the Stolen Generations.

    Cultural safety. This file names the taking of children, because Neville said it, and because the 1905 Act made the Chief Protector the legal guardian of children. It does not describe acts of violence in detail, it does not print photographs of children, and it does not invent an Aboriginal child’s voice. Students should not be asked to role-play a removal, or to write a first-person “Stolen Generations” monologue. If a class is not ready for the 1937 sentences, stop at What is agreed and return later. Offer students a way to step out. The language of the 1905 Act and of Neville’s 1937 speech is racist in its official categories. It is reproduced here as evidence, clearly attributed, not as this file’s voice.

    Flags. The 1787 manuscript wording (“Savages” / “Natives”) is flagged above. Section 8 as reserved in 1905 is not identical with later reprints that include the 1911 mother-exclusion words. William Harris’s exact spoken sentences were not checked word for word against The West Australian and The Daily News in this build, so they are not printed as quotations on the student page. Collier’s “great obligation” line should be checked in those papers before it is taught as a confirmed quotation.
